.shot > img · .ring · .patch

Screenshot

The media frame: a real product screen, or a clip of one (next sections), at its natural pixel size, scaled down by CSS, with 6px rounding, a soft shadow and no border. With kit.js it fades in when it loads and opens in the lightbox on click. Annotations are children positioned in percent of the image box, so they hold at any rendered size. A .ring marks one element and is drawn 8px outside it with a wide glow, so it reads as an overlay rather than a focus state; .ring--pill follows a pill-shaped control. A .patch paints over a region while a post is in review; once it settles, the same rectangle goes into the post's images.json and the pipeline bakes it into the asset, so the lightbox and any 2x file carry the fix too.

Lightbox switches. data-full="…" on an image opens a larger file than the one shown inline. data-no-lightbox opts out: on an image, on a wrapper such as a figure or a grid, on .blog-content for a whole post, or on <html> to disable it for the page. The wide panel below carries it, so that screen does not open; the cursor follows.

.panel · .panel--dark · .panel--bleed · .panel--wide

Panel

The surface for anything cut out of its screen: a dialog, a detail, or a whole screen or clip captured without a browser frame. It holds one .shot, still or clip. A window capture that frames itself takes the column bare; everything else sits on a panel. At the default width it holds a dialog or a detail, at most 520px wide. --bleed makes the image the panel: edge to edge, no padding, no shadow, no lightbox, for a capture whose own background does the framing. --wide lets a whole screen fill the panel with a small margin, which is how a capture or a recording with no browser frame gets one; it needs the full row, so it goes in a .wide block. Light is the default. --dark runs from the product teal into steel blue and is the highlight: when a section has several panels, at most one is dark, and it is the one that matters most.

.compare · .compare--vertical

Before and after

Two captures of the same screen, revealed with a drag handle; the tags name the two versions. Both captures must share window size, zoom and scroll position, or the handle reveals differences that are not the point. The logic is the one the use-case pages already ship; the kit only restyles the handle and adds the tags. The --vertical variant turns the divider horizontal and drags up and down; it exists on request, for a toolbar or a header row that changed. The default is the one to reach for: screens are laid out in columns, so a vertical divider cuts between regions, while a horizontal one runs through every widget in a row.

.shot > video · .panel .shot

Looped clip

A clip is a .shot whose child is a <video> instead of an image: the same frame, the same two placements. On its own it takes the column, for a window capture that frames itself. Inside a .panel, light or dark, it gets the panel's margin and gradient, which is how a recording with no browser bar gets a frame: --wide for a whole screen, the default width for a dialog-sized clip. It autoplays muted, pauses when scrolled out of view, is not a lightbox target, and under reduced motion shows the poster with a play button. Files come out of tools/prepare-video.sh: H.264 and VP9 with no audio, scaled to twice the column, plus a poster; the script also crops to the app window and trims a slow start or a cursor dash at the end.

.grid · .block · .sub · .wide

Layouts

One grid for anything side by side: two columns by default, three with --3, a tighter gap with --tight, and .wide on a child to span the row. A block is a small heading, a sentence and a visual, with no chrome around it. Everything stacks to one column on phones. The Panel section above is a grid of three blocks and one wide one.

.stepper · .card--muted · .num

Steps

Two ways to show a real sequence, where the order is information. The stepper is the horizontal one: number, connector, title and a short line, stacking to a vertical rail on phones. The numbered cards are the roomier version for steps that need a sentence or two. The vertical stepper, below under For review, is the rail at every width, for steps that each carry a paragraph and a visual.

For review

Elements the live blog builds by hand today, from a survey of the 51 posts: notes as bold “Tip:” paragraphs or a one-off component, quotes as the template's tinted blockquote, feature rows with inline styles, tables that overflow the column, “Step N” headings, interviews as bold “Q:” paragraphs, and nothing at the top of a release post that says what is in it. One class each, the same tokens. Nothing below is used in a post yet; it is here to be looked at, kept or dropped.

.note · .note--warn · .note__label

Note review

A short aside the reader may skip: a tip, a requirement, a version caveat. One tinted box with a left rule, an optional uppercase label, and a warning variant in the notice colour. It replaces the bold “Tip:” paragraph, the one-off BlogNote and the blockquote-as-note; a quotation is not a note (next section).

.quote · cite

Quote review

A pull quote or a customer's words, with the attribution under them. Larger type and a quiet rule, no tint: the register of a quotation, not of a note. Customer and partner posts use these; the kit keeps the markup to a blockquote with a cite.

Feature row review

One feature per row: a heading and a sentence beside a visual, sides alternating with --flip. For a product post that walks through a few features one at a time, where a grid of tiles would crowd them; the visual is any kit visual, here a panel each. Stacks to one column on phones, text first.

.table-wrap · .table--compare

Table review

The template styles tables but gives them no room to overflow, so a wide one breaks the column on phones. The wrapper scrolls it sideways instead. The compare variant makes the first column the row header and centres the values, for edition and version comparisons; a plain table keeps the template's left alignment.

.qa · dt · dd

Interview review

A question-and-answer post as a definition list: the question carries a mark, the answer sits under it. The two interview posts do this with bold “Q:” paragraphs today.

What changed in how Edge talks to the cloud?

Nothing on the wire. What changed is who installs and updates it: the agent does, from the cloud side, so an operator never opens a terminal on the site.

Does that work behind a firewall?

Yes. The agent opens the connection outward, the same way Edge always has.
